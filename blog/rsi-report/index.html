Is AI Improving Itself?

A Survey of Claims, Evidence, and Empirical Tests on Recursive Self-Improvement

AI that helps with research is not yet AI that has learned to speed up its own research.

Current AI R&D results show that AI can help improve models, code and training pipelines, but this is not yet evidence of recursive self-improvement. Supporting an RSI claim further requires showing that a successor system can rewrite its own improvement mechanism and that its speed of improvement keeps rising in comparisons across generations. A review of existing research and public claims finds such evidence still scarce; this report's experiment likewise finds that single-generation AI-assisted research works, but training on the model's own experience, or letting the model design its own update recipe, brought no reliable speed-up across generations.

AI systems now help develop models, code, and training pipelines. But helping improve an object is different from making the improver itself better at improving. I use *recursive self-improvement* (RSI) for the stronger claim: a system can change its own improvement mechanism, and successive generations increase the speed of improvement3,4,5.

I review theoretical definitions, related academic work, and four public reports from Recursive, Naive, and Apex. These reports describe AI improving training code, inference systems, model architectures, or training data. They do not show whether a successor becomes a faster improver. Self-training studies generally report changes in model performance rather than generation-to-generation improvement speed. This review found no controlled measurement of this kind at the level of model weights6,7.

To measure the gap, Qwen3.5-9B first conducted research on NanoChat. Successors trained on its records were then compared on eight unseen micro-tasks. The fixed original model achieved a clear first-order improvement, lowering NanoChat validation loss from 1.4152 to 1.2308. The cross-generation result was different: none of the three successors improved faster than the original, and fine-tuning on accepted records did not outperform a placebo trained on rejected records1,2.

A lessons note written from the model's own experience reduced improvement speed by about a third and underperformed generic advice on all eight tasks. Letting the model design its training recipe also produced no reliable acceleration. A new-seed rerun of the Round-1 recipe scored 0.602, close to its original 0.605, so ordinary seed variation does not explain Round 3's 0.392. A recipe designed by Qwen3.8-27B scored 0.513, still below the original model1,2.

AI-assisted research can produce first-order gains. Evidence of recursive improvement requires successors to advance research faster than their predecessors. This study measures improvement at the model-weight level and finds successor performance sensitive to data selection and training intensity. An update succeeds when the resulting researcher improves faster.

01

What counts as RSI

In 1965, I. J. Good argued that a machine capable of surpassing humans across intellectual tasks should also be able to design better machines. Successors repeating this process could trigger an "intelligence explosion"8. The idea became known as recursive self-improvement (RSI), but researchers still disagree on its boundaries. An ICLR workshop devoted to RSI in 2026 likewise stopped short of a general formal definition9.

The central question is whether the improvement loop returns to the improver. Yudkowsky distinguishes a system optimizing itself from one system optimizing a separate object10,4. A fixed model that repeatedly improves training code or a target model makes first-order research progress. The loop becomes recursive when an updated system takes over as the researcher and changes its own capacity to make further improvements. Self-modification alone does not guarantee continued progress. Early examples such as EURISKO and optimizing compilers improved themselves before stalling10.

Two questions are often bundled together: who controls the improvement mechanism, and whether improvement accelerates across generations. Tang et al. use the first as a formal boundary. When data selection, training recipes, or other mechanisms remain outside the agent, the process is general iterative policy improvement (GPI). It qualifies as RSI under their definition only when the system can internalize and rewrite those mechanisms5. This definition answers what counts as RSI, not whether RSI works.

Effectiveness requires a separate measurement. Yampolskiy distinguishes self-modification, weak self-improvement, and recursive self-improvement4. The highest level requires more than tuning the system. The system must get better at getting better by improving how it produces successors. Chalmers calls the relevant capacity self-amplifying capacity, the capacity to produce the next generation11. When people fix the training process in advance, self-distillation and self-play task scores support a first-order performance claim. Recursive improvement requires evidence that the update mechanism itself strengthens across generations (an inference)12,4,5.

This report separates the evidence into two levels. First-order improvement occurs when a fixed researcher improves a target under a given budget. Qwen3.5-9B lowering NanoChat validation loss from 1.4152 to 1.2308 is a first-order result1. Cross-generation improvement compares the speed of successor researchers under the same tasks and budget. A successor that improves faster than its predecessor provides direct evidence that the capacity to improve is growing. Weco calls this threshold ignition, and AIDE² operationalizes it with an ignition test13,6. HGM measures an agent's capacity to produce stronger descendants as metaproductivity14.

Macro-level work also uses the R&D return parameter r to describe the relation between research input and efficiency. Epoch and Forethought define r in terms of the efficiency gain associated with a doubling of R&D input. Values above 1 correspond to increasingly large efficiency gains per doubling16,17. Historical estimates vary widely, and no direct estimate is available for LLM research17. This report measures generational improvement speed under a fixed budget, which cannot be converted directly into r, defined over cumulative research input. The two measures address acceleration but quantify different things.

02

The four releases

In 2026, Recursive, Naive, and Apex published four reports on AI-assisted R&D. They offer distinct views of current AI4AI work, including automated search, inference optimization, model training, and model-designed training procedures.

Recursive: automated search and model development

First Steps applies automated search to NanoChat training code, NanoGPT Speedrun, and GPU kernels. It reports lowering NanoChat validation loss from 0.9372 to 0.9109, reducing Speedrun time from 79.7 to 77.5 seconds, and raising the mean SOL-ExecBench score from 0.699 to 0.75424,25,26,27,28,29,30,31,32. These metrics span training scripts, program speed, and kernel optimization.

Flint extends automated R&D to model development. Recursive's Eureka Machine helped train a language model from scratch, and the report gives its scale, training timeline, and inference efficiency33,34,35,36. Having Flint take over development of Eureka Machine remains future work21.

Naive: from research attempts to system results

NaiveRT's logs preserve 151 optimization attempts and report reducing speculative decoding time from 12.3 to 3.4 milliseconds37,38,39,40. AutoWM presents a system-level result: the world model developed with N0.5-Flash scored 77.43 on WorldArena41,42. One report foregrounds the optimization process, the other the benchmark performance of a model developed with AI assistance.

Apex: the label and the generational evidence

Apex describes its AI4AI work as "recursive self-improvement"43,44. Its Sokoban case reports that an Apex-designed training procedure raised Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct's success rate from 11.7% to 24.4% on 512 levels45,46,47. This is a result from a model designing and running a reinforcement-learning experiment. Testing generational recursion requires measuring whether a successor Apex can take over and design effective training faster than its predecessor.

Together, the reports document AI contributions to training scripts, kernels, inference systems, and training procedures. The next result considered here is the one they do not directly compare: the improvement speed of successor researchers.

03

The missing measurement

Current benchmarks mostly measure whether a model can perform research tasks. RE-Bench evaluates machine-learning engineering, MLE-bench tracks medals in Kaggle competitions, and PaperBench tests paper reproduction48,49,50. They answer what a model can do now. They do not answer whether a successor produced through research can do the same work faster. METR estimates that Anthropic's overall R&D pace has increased by about 1.9 times, but that figure includes human researchers and cannot isolate the effect of model succession51.

A direct test of recursive improvement places a successor back in the same research process, gives it the same tasks and budget, and compares its speed of improvement with its predecessor. Existing work reaches different parts of this test:

The literature has two broad approaches. Self-training changes model weights, but researchers usually specify the update rule in advance. Code-modifying systems can change the search procedure, but often keep the underlying language model fixed. AIDE² is among the few studies to test whether an improver can improve itself, but it modifies the agent scaffold rather than the language-model weights6. Whether a model can use its own research records to become a stronger researcher therefore remains an open empirical question.

The measurement is difficult for practical reasons. Inner research runs are expensive, and outer comparisons must be repeated across generations. Noise compounds across both stages. If the improver can alter the evaluator, a higher score may reflect metric exploitation rather than better research. STOP, DGM, and METR document examples of this problem58,56,60. A meaningful comparison therefore needs fixed tasks and budgets, plus an evaluator isolated from the improver.

Section 4 tests this question at the level of model weights. The original model and its successors work on the same unseen tasks under a fixed budget, with a repeated baseline, a rejected-record placebo, and an equal-data control.

04

Experiment: swapping in the successor

Tasks and measurement

The initial improver was Qwen3.5-9B. The first task was NanoChat: the model edited the training script, and an evaluator trained each candidate under a fixed budget and returned its validation loss. The second suite contained eight unseen micro-research tasks, where the model reduced validation loss by adjusting training configurations. A separate set of eight training tasks supplied trajectories and lessons notes but did not enter the final comparisons1,61,62.

On the unseen tasks, each improver made 12 attempts, using the same five random seeds for each task. The improvement-speed score measures the average fraction of the gap between the starting performance and a reference optimum closed over those attempts. It ranges from 0 to 1, with higher scores indicating more progress within the fixed budget. Comparisons are paired by task, confidence intervals use bootstrap, and p-values come from paired sign-flip tests62.

Controls distinguish learning useful research judgment from changing the data or the model's output habits. They include an A/A rerun of the original model, a placebo trained on rejected records, equal-volume data produced by the original model, and random recipes. The decision rule was set in advance: a positive difference, a confidence interval excluding zero, an effect larger than A/A variation, and an advantage over the matched control were all required to detect second-order improvement62,63.

A fixed improver: clear first-order progress

Qwen3.5-9B ran for 176.6 minutes and proposed and evaluated 72 attempts, lowering NanoChat validation loss by 0.1844, from 1.4152 to 1.23081. Evaluation noise does not account for this change. Because the improver remained fixed, this establishes first-order progress. Cross-generation change requires a comparison between successors.

Lessons note: local experience became a general rule

After reading records from eight training tasks, the model wrote a 252-word lessons note. It prescribed a fixed order for adjusting hyperparameters and advised: “Ignore changes that increase loss even slightly.” Adding the note to the prompt lowered improvement speed from 0.588 to 0.392, a difference of −0.196 (95% CI [−0.383, −0.033]). Equal-length generic advice scored about 0.631. The lessons note trailed this control on all eight tasks, by a mean of −0.239 (95% CI [−0.420, −0.075], p=0.008)63.

The largest drops occurred on 2-D classification, sequence reversal, and in-context linear regression. This pattern is consistent with the note turning local successes on the training tasks into a fixed search order. The experience did not help the model judge new tasks. It led the model to rule out potentially useful exploration earlier. Experience can guide cross-task search when its rules have been independently validated.

Weight updates: accepted records did not beat rejected records

Generation 1 was fine-tuned on research records accepted by the original model. Its placebo was trained on an equal number of rejected records under the same settings. The two models were nearly identical on unseen tasks: Generation 1 was ahead by 0.004 (95% CI [−0.084, 0.080], p=0.91). This comparison offers no evidence that the model learned which research hypotheses were effective from these records.

Generations 2 and 3 tested whether an updated model could produce more useful records for the next successor. To control data volume, the successor and original-model control received equal numbers of new records, generated by different models. The original and three successors scored 0.588, 0.537, 0.573, and 0.487. Generation 2 returned close to baseline, Generation 3 scored 0.087 below Generation 2 (95% CI [−0.190, −0.005], sign-flip p=0.0625). No generation exceeded the original model1,61,63.

Across three generations, no repeatable cross-generation acceleration appeared. Generation 1's similarity to the rejected-record placebo also shows that converting model-generated research traces into training data did not teach the model to distinguish effective from ineffective research decisions.

Self-designed recipes: intuition did not identify a better update

The model then chose data filters and LoRA settings from a predefined option space. The original model's first recipe scored 0.605, a second recipe designed after seeing one round of training-task feedback scored 0.595, a third recipe designed by the first fine-tuned model scored 0.392. None exceeded the original model's 0.588. The third recipe scored 0.204 below the second (95% CI [−0.365, −0.065], p=0.008)1,63.

The third recipe resembled the first but added a filter that kept only records with at least 1% relative gain. Retraining the first recipe with a new seed yielded 0.602, close to its original 0.605, so ordinary seed variation cannot explain the gap between rounds 1 and 3. Qwen3.8-27B used the same filter with lighter training and scored 0.513. It did better than round 3 but remained below the original and round-2 models1,2.

The recipe comparisons show that data selection and training intensity both affect successor performance. The settings do not isolate each factor. Successor performance is the criterion for judging a recipe.

Continuing from the NanoChat frontier

The original model, Generation 1, the rejected-record placebo, and the model with the lessons note continued from the same best NanoChat checkpoint for 12 attempts, with three runs per group. Their mean loss reductions were 0.0124, 0.0203, 0.0178, and 0.0110. Group differences were not statistically significant64.

Under these conditions, lessons notes, trajectory fine-tuning, and self-designed recipes did not increase successor improvement speed. Successor research ability should be evaluated on new tasks.

05

Against the literature

Experience transfers only when it is validated

The model-written lessons note lowered improvement speed, echoing negative results in studies of reflection and memory. ExpeL, Reflexion, and ACE report performance drops when reflections or context rewrites lack reliable external filtering65,66,67. Positive results tend to tie experience to verifiable artifacts: Sarukkai et al. retrieve successful trajectories, while Voyager stores executable skills that pass self-verification68,69.

Useful memories can be checked against the environment and matched to the task at hand. The note in Section 4 turned local training experience into a cross-task rule without testing it on new tasks, narrowing the search space. An experience store should preserve reproducible artifacts with task conditions and external outcomes.

Accepted records did not teach research judgment

The first successor fine-tuned on accepted records performed almost identically to the rejected-record placebo. This resembles Spurious Rewards, where random rewards captured most of the gain of true rewards on MATH-50072. Mind the Gap likewise found that some apparent first-round self-improvement came from better format following rather than task ability73. A score increase after an update does not show that the model learned useful information from feedback. Data volume, formatting, and behavior activation require controls.

Rejected records serve as a placebo, separating generic effects of training from gains due to useful research signals. Fine-tuning on accepted records did not produce faster research progress in this experiment.

Task scores and improvement ability measure different outcomes

Many self-training studies report model performance on the target task. SPIN and Self-Rewarding show that some metrics continue to rise across iterations, while also reporting diminishing gains or regressions on other tasks54,74. These findings matter, but they describe changes in task performance. They do not directly test whether an updated model can improve the next task faster.

Agent-level work offers adjacent but distinct evidence. DGM shows that letting an improved agent perform the next round of code edits can raise the final result56. AIDE²'s ignition test directly compares an evolved coach with a hand-built coach, but does not distinguish them6. These studies shift attention from a model's isolated output to the R&D loop, yet their targets, update mechanisms, and budgets differ. Their scores cannot be compared numerically with weight-level improvement-speed scores.

Macro models of R&D returns describe the relation between cumulative input and software efficiency. Epoch and Forethought use r to represent the efficiency gain associated with a doubling of research input. Historical estimates vary across fields, and there is no direct estimate for LLM research16,17. This report fixes the budget per run and compares improvement speed across generations. That is not the same quantity as r, which is defined over cumulative input. The null result here therefore cannot be converted into an estimate of r. It does answer a more local question: under these conditions, did a successor make the research loop faster?

Section 4 compares fine-tuned researchers under fixed tasks and budgets, with A/A, rejected-trajectory, equal-volume data, and random-recipe controls. Further work can extend the model and task range, add independent training runs, and optimize successor research speed.
